Historic trend — the world ocean sink

Annual ocean CO₂ uptake, world total, 1959–2024 (GtC per year). The sink has roughly tripled since 1959, tracking rising atmospheric CO₂ — but it varies year to year with El Niño, volcanic cooling and ocean circulation. Source: Global Carbon Budget 2025.

The four pumps — the science behind the numbers

Every tonne in the table below got into the ocean through one of these four pumps, and they work together — the Global ONCE framework calls their coupling BCMS. Source: Global Ocean Negative Carbon Emissions Program (Global ONCE) Science Plan, Chief Scientist Nianzhi Jiao. Gt = 1 Pg.

What the numbers can and cannot say: no published source splits any basin's uptake into pump shares, so this page does not invent one. Each basin's drivers are attributed qualitatively — which pumps dominate there, and why — with the source cited. The one hard number on the counter-pump is its per-mole factor: about 0.6 mol of CO₂ released per mole of CaCO₃ precipitated (Frankignoulle et al. 1994), a ratio that rises as seawater loses buffer capacity.

Warming pushes back on the solubility pump

Surface-ocean pCO₂ rises about 4.23% for every 1 °C of warming (Takahashi et al. 1993), which shrinks the air–sea gradient that drives uptake. In 2023, the hottest year on record for sea-surface temperature, the non-polar ocean absorbed about 10% less carbon than expected — a shortfall of roughly 0.16 PgC — concentrated in the extratropics, above all the North Atlantic (Müller et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change). The same warming did not stop the sink growing over decades, because rising atmospheric CO₂ keeps steepening the gradient; the two effects pull in opposite directions, and the balance is what the annual series measures.

How to read the columns. Uptake: how much CO₂ the basin takes up in a year, in petagrams of carbon (1 PgC = 1 GtC = 3.664 GtCO₂). Share: the basin's slice of the eight-basin total printed here. Area: the ocean surface the Fay climatology covers in that basin — 322 million km² across all eight, of about 361 million km² of world ocean; coasts, the Arctic and marginal seas are not covered. Flux per area: uptake divided by area — the fair way to compare a giant basin with a small intense one. Dominant pumps: which of the four pumps do most of the work there (see the pump section above).

Reconciliation: the eight basin values sum to 1.88 PgC/yr of uptake, against the paper's global open-ocean net flux of 1.79 ± 0.7 PgC/yr. The 0.09 gap is rounding in the printed table (values given to 0.01) and coverage differences; it sits well inside the stated uncertainty. Basin shares are shares of the printed-basin total, so they sum to 100%.

Net flux vs the anthropogenic sink — read this before comparing the two halves of the page. The basin table is a net air–sea flux (−1.79 PgC/yr globally): it counts everything crossing the surface, including the natural outgassing of carbon that rivers carry to the sea (about 0.65 GtC/yr). The annual global series is the anthropogenic sink (3.39 GtC/yr in 2024): the extra uptake caused by rising atmospheric CO₂, after the river outgassing is added back. The two are consistent once that adjustment is made — the GCB applies exactly it — and the basin table is centred near 2010, when the anthropogenic sink was about 2.5 GtC/yr, not today's 3.4.

The variables that watch it — GOOS Essential Ocean Variables

The ocean sink is not measured by one instrument. It is constrained by this set of 36 Essential Ocean Variables (EOVs) — the minimum set needed to assess ocean state and change, coordinated by the Global Ocean Observing System (GOOS). Source: Martín Míguez, Heslop et al. 2026, Frontiers in Marine Science 13:1737002 (CC BY 4.0). ECV overlap verified against the GCOS Essential Climate Variables list and the paper's section 3.2.1.

VariableGOOS categoryPilotAlso an Essential Climate Variable (ECV)?

How to read the ECV column. 19 of the GCOS Essential Climate Variables are ocean-focused. Eleven are physics EOVs and five are biogeochemistry EOVs; ocean colour is an ECV in its own right; and two biological ECVs are compounds of several EOVs — the Marine Habitats ECV is mangroves + seagrass + macroalgal canopy + coral, and the Plankton ECV is phytoplankton + zooplankton. Note on ocean colour: the poster colours it with the physical set, but the paper classes it as cross-disciplinary; the paper's classification is used here. Pilot EOVs (turbulent diapycnal fluxes, microbes, benthic invertebrates) are still being established as sustained observations.

Which variables constrain the uptake?

The sink is pinned down first by inorganic carbon (whose sub-variable is surface-ocean pCO₂ — the direct measure of the air–sea gradient), sea surface temperature and sea surface salinity (which set solubility and stratification), sea state (wind and waves drive gas transfer), ocean colour and phytoplankton / zooplankton (the biological pump at work), oxygen and nutrients (what biology consumes and leaves behind), stable carbon isotopes (which separate natural from human carbon), and dissolved organic carbon and particulate matter (what the microbial and biological pumps move). Each basin's detail panel lists the EOVs that watch that basin. Every EOV named on this page carries its GOOS category and the Martín Míguez et al. 2026 citation.

Methods, model assumptions & sources

What is counted

The headline global figure is the Global Carbon Budget ocean sink (SOCEAN): the net uptake of CO₂ by the ocean caused by rising atmospheric CO₂, in GtC per year. It is the average of 10 global ocean biogeochemistry models and 9 surface-ocean fCO₂ data products, with an uncertainty of ±0.4 GtC/yr. The basin figures are the Fay et al. 2024 climatological mean net air–sea fluxes by open-ocean biome, aggregated to eight basins.

What is not in the headline figure

The basin table does not include coastal oceans (within 100 km of land), the Arctic Ocean, or marginal seas — the Fay climatology excludes them, which is why its covered area (322 M km²) is smaller than the world ocean (~361 M km²). The global series does cover the whole ocean. Land-use change emissions are not part of the sink; they are shown only as the denominator of the "share of emissions" figures.

Model & estimate assumptions

  • Live counter: the 2024 ocean rate (12.42 GtCO₂/year) is spread evenly across the year — 394 tonnes of CO₂ per second — and applied to 2026 until a newer annual total is published. It is an accounting spread, not a real-time measurement; real uptake varies by season, weather and El Niño.
  • Cumulative live total: cumulative ocean uptake at the end of 2024 (146.0 GtC) plus the preliminary 2025 estimate (3.11 GtC) plus the 2026 estimate from 1 January to now, at the same even rate.
  • Basin fluxes: Fay et al. fluxes are computed from SOCATv2022 observations for 1980–2021 with 2010 inputs (atmospheric CO₂, winds); they represent a multi-decade mean centred near 2010, not any single year. Per-area fluxes are the printed flux divided by the printed area. Basin shares are shares of the eight printed basin values, so they sum to 100%.
  • Pump attribution: qualitative only. No published source splits a basin's uptake into pump shares; the page attributes dominant pumps per basin from the cited driver descriptions and says so where a split is not available.
  • Rounding: figures are shown to two decimals (GtC) or one decimal (PgC) as shown; underlying values are in data.json.
